Payment reconciliation, explained.

Three records should describe the same money. Reconciliation is how you prove they do, and find out why when they do not.

A MoneyBee guide. Last updated 23 November 2026

The short answer

Payment reconciliation is checking that the money you were owed is the money you received. For card and online payments that means comparing three records of the same activity and explaining every difference.

The three records

RecordWho produces itWhat it tells you
Your own recordsYour order system, point of sale or ledgerWhat you sold and what you expected to be paid
The processor reportYour acquirer or payment providerWhat was approved, refunded or disputed, and the fees taken
The bank statementYour bankWhat actually arrived in your account, and when

Matching all three is called three-way matching. Matching only two leaves a blind spot. If you compare orders with the processor report alone, you never confirm the money reached the bank.

Why the three never agree on their own

  • Timing. A payment taken on Thursday may settle the following week. Weekends and public holidays move settlement dates.
  • Net settlement. Providers usually pay out one amount per day or per batch, after taking their fees. One bank line can stand for hundreds of sales.
  • Fees. The fee on each payment depends on the card and the method, so the deduction is rarely a round percentage.
  • Refunds and chargebacks. These reduce a later payout, often on a different day from the original sale.
  • Currency. A sale in one currency settled in another arrives as a different number.
  • More than one provider. Each provider has its own report format, cut-off time and settlement cycle.

How three-way matching works

  1. Collect the three records for the same period.
  2. Match each sale in your records to a line in the processor report, using a shared reference.
  3. Group processor lines into the payout they belong to, and subtract fees, refunds and chargebacks.
  4. Match each payout to a line on the bank statement.
  5. Review what is left. Every unmatched item is a question: a payment not yet settled, a fee you did not expect, a refund you did not record, or money that is missing.

What good reconciliation gives you

  • You find missing or short payouts while they can still be queried.
  • You know the real cost of each payment method and provider.
  • Month-end closes faster, because differences are explained daily and not all at once.
  • Your auditors can trace any bank line back to the sales behind it.

Doing it by hand, and when to stop

A small business with one provider can reconcile in a spreadsheet: download the processor report, download the bank statement, and tick lines off. It becomes unworkable when volumes grow, when a second provider is added, or when refunds and chargebacks are frequent. At that point teams look for software that reads the reports and matches them automatically, leaving people to review only the exceptions.

Questions to ask about any reconciliation tool

  • Does it read my providers' reports and my bank's statements directly, or do I upload files?
  • Does it match at the level of each payment, or only each payout?
  • Does it show fees per payment?
  • How does it handle refunds, chargebacks and partial settlements?
  • What happens to an item it cannot match?
